How to see the planets in the night sky

You don't need expensive gear to see other worlds. With a little timing and a dark sky, five planets are visible with nothing but your eyes.

By Orbitra TeamPublished 20 November 20266 min read

Planets vs. stars: how to tell the difference

Stars are distant suns, so far away that they appear as single points of light. As that light passes through our turbulent atmosphere, it shimmers — the familiar twinkle. Planets are much closer and appear as tiny discs, so their light is far steadier.

Planets also move. Over days and weeks they drift against the background stars, which is how they earned their name: the Greek planētēs means "wanderer". They always stay close to the ecliptic, the path the Sun traces across our sky.

The five naked-eye planets

PlanetWhat it looks likeBest tip
MercurySmall, pinkish point low on the horizonLook just after sunset or before sunrise
VenusBrilliant white "evening" or "morning star"The brightest object after the Sun and Moon
MarsDistinctly orange-red, steady lightBrightest around opposition, every ~26 months
JupiterVery bright, creamy whiteBinoculars show up to four moons beside it
SaturnGolden, fainter than JupiterA small telescope reveals its rings

Uranus is technically at the limit of naked-eye visibility under very dark skies, while Neptune always requires binoculars or a telescope.

Step-by-step: your first planet hunt

  1. Check what is up tonight. Free planetarium software such as Stellarium shows exactly which planets are above your horizon.
  2. Find a dark location. Get away from street lights and pick a spot with a low, open horizon — especially for Mercury and Venus.
  3. Let your eyes adapt. Put the phone away for around 20 minutes. Use a red light if you need to read a chart.
  4. Follow the ecliptic. Imagine a line joining the setting Sun and the Moon; the planets will be strung along it.
  5. Look for the steady lights. A bright point that refuses to twinkle is almost certainly a planet.
Tip: the Moon is a great signpost. When it passes close to a bright planet — an event called a conjunction — finding that planet becomes effortless.

Does equipment help?

Binoculars

A pair of 7×50 or 10×50 binoculars is the best first upgrade. Held steady, they reveal Jupiter's four Galilean moons — the same view Galileo had in 1610 — and the crescent phase of Venus when it is near the Sun.

A small telescope

A modest telescope of 70–100 mm aperture shows Saturn's rings, the cloud belts of Jupiter and, during a close approach, dark markings on Mars. Steadiness and patience matter more than magnification.
