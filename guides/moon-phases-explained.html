The Moon guide

Moon phases explained

The Moon doesn't change shape — our view of its sunlit half does. Here's how the 29.5-day cycle works and how to make the most of each phase.

By Orbitra TeamPublished 20 November 20265 min read

Why the Moon has phases

The Sun always lights up half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, about 384,400 km away, we see different proportions of that sunlit half. When the lit portion grows, the Moon is waxing; when it shrinks, it is waning.

A complete cycle from New Moon to New Moon — the synodic month — takes about 29.5 days. That is slightly longer than the 27.3 days the Moon takes to orbit Earth, because Earth is also moving around the Sun.

The eight phases in order

  1. New Moon. The Moon sits between Earth and the Sun, so its sunlit side faces away from us and it is invisible.
  2. Waxing crescent. A thin sliver appears in the western sky after sunset, growing night by night.
  3. First quarter. Half of the disc is lit. Craters along the terminator — the line between day and night — look especially dramatic.
  4. Waxing gibbous. More than half is lit and growing. The Moon rises in the afternoon and is visible most of the evening.
  5. Full Moon. Earth lies between the Sun and Moon, and the entire face is lit. It rises at sunset and sets at sunrise.
  6. Waning gibbous. The lit portion shrinks, and the Moon rises later each night.
  7. Third quarter. The opposite half is lit. It rises around midnight and is high in the morning sky.
  8. Waning crescent. A thin crescent visible before dawn in the east, shrinking toward the next New Moon.

Why we always see the same side

The Moon is tidally locked: it rotates once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to orbit Earth, so the same hemisphere always faces us. The "far side" is not permanently dark — it receives just as much sunlight; we simply never see it from Earth.

Phases and eclipses

A solar eclipse can only happen at New Moon, when the Moon passes in front of the Sun. A lunar eclipse can only happen at Full Moon, when the Moon moves through Earth's shadow. Eclipses don't happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° relative to Earth's.

Best time to observe the Moon

Surprisingly, the Full Moon is the worst time to look at craters: sunlight hits the surface head-on, so there are no shadows. The best views come around first and third quarter, when long shadows along the terminator make mountains and craters stand out in sharp relief.

For daily Moon phase data and imagery, see NASA's Moon page and NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio.

Frequently asked questions

How long does each Moon phase last?

The full cycle takes about 29.5 days. The four principal phases (New, First Quarter, Full, Third Quarter) are instants; the in-between phases each last roughly a week.

What is a blue moon?

Commonly, a blue moon is the second Full Moon in a single calendar month. It happens about once every two to three years.

Is the Moon's far side dark?

No. The far side gets the same amount of sunlight as the near side; it is simply always turned away from Earth.
