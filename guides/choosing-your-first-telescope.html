Choosing your first telescope

The right first telescope is the one you will actually use. Here's how to understand the jargon and pick an instrument that suits your sky and your patience.

By Orbitra TeamPublished 20 November 20268 min read
In this guide

  2. Aperture is king
  3. The three main designs
  4. Mounts matter
  5. Understanding magnification
  6. Common beginner mistakes

Start with binoculars

Before buying a telescope, try a pair of 7×50 or 10×50 binoculars. They are easy to use, show a wide field of view and reveal Jupiter's moons, lunar craters, star clusters and the Andromeda Galaxy. The first number is magnification; the second is the diameter of each front lens in millimetres.

Aperture is king

A telescope's most important specification is its aperture — the diameter of its main lens or mirror. A larger aperture gathers more light, revealing fainter objects and finer detail. Doubling the aperture collects four times as much light.

Rule of thumb: buy the largest aperture you can comfortably carry, set up and store. A big telescope that stays in the cupboard shows you nothing.

The three main designs

TypeHow it worksBest for
RefractorUses lenses to bend light to a focus.Sharp views of the Moon and planets; low maintenance.
Reflector (Newtonian)Uses a curved primary mirror.The most aperture for the money; galaxies and nebulae.
CatadioptricCombines mirrors and lenses in a compact tube.Portability and long focal lengths for planets.

A Dobsonian is a Newtonian reflector on a simple rocking base. Many astronomers consider it the best-value beginner choice because it offers large aperture and intuitive point-and-look use.

Mounts matter

A wobbly mount ruins any telescope. Alt-azimuth mounts move up-down and left-right and are intuitive for beginners. Equatorial mounts are aligned with Earth's axis, making it easier to follow objects as the sky turns and essential for long-exposure astrophotography. Computerised "GoTo" mounts can find thousands of objects automatically.

Understanding magnification

Magnification depends on the eyepiece you use:

Magnification = telescope focal length ÷ eyepiece focal length
Example: a 1,200 mm telescope with a 25 mm eyepiece gives 48×.

The highest useful magnification is roughly twice the aperture in millimetres — about 200× for a 100 mm telescope — and our turbulent atmosphere often limits views to less. Be wary of any telescope advertised mainly by an enormous magnification figure.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Chasing magnification. Low power shows brighter, sharper and wider views.
  • Skipping the finder alignment. Align your finder scope on a distant object in daylight first.
  • Not letting it cool. Put the telescope outside 30 minutes before observing so the optics reach outdoor temperature.
  • Pointing at the Sun. Never look at the Sun through any optical instrument without a certified solar filter — it causes instant, permanent eye damage.

Local astronomy clubs often run free "star parties" where you can try different telescopes before choosing one. The International Astronomical Union's public outreach pages are a good place to start.

Frequently asked questions

What size telescope should a beginner get?

A reflector or Dobsonian of 114–200 mm aperture, or a 70–100 mm refractor, is a good starting point. Choose something you can carry and set up easily.

Can I see galaxies with a small telescope?

Yes. Bright galaxies such as Andromeda are visible even in binoculars, though they appear as faint smudges rather than the colourful spirals seen in long-exposure photographs.

What magnification do I need to see Saturn's rings?

Around 25× is enough to show the rings; 75–150× gives a more detailed view on steady nights.
