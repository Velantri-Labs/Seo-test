Glossary

Astronomy terms, made simple

Twenty-four essential words every stargazer meets sooner or later — explained clearly, without the jargon.

ACDEGHIKLMOPRT

A

Albedo
The fraction of sunlight a surface reflects. Fresh snow has a high albedo; charcoal-dark asteroids have a very low one.
Aphelion
The point in a planet's orbit where it is farthest from the Sun. Earth reaches aphelion in early July.
Asteroid belt
A region between Mars and Jupiter containing millions of rocky bodies, including the dwarf planet Ceres.
Astronomical unit (AU)
The average distance between Earth and the Sun, about 149.6 million kilometres. Used to measure distances within the Solar System.

C

Conjunction
When two objects appear close together in the sky as seen from Earth, such as the Moon passing near a bright planet.

D

Dwarf planet
A body that orbits the Sun and is massive enough to be round, but has not cleared its orbital neighbourhood. Pluto, Ceres and Eris are examples.

E

Eclipse
When one celestial body moves into the shadow of another. In a solar eclipse the Moon blocks the Sun; in a lunar eclipse Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.
Ecliptic
The apparent path of the Sun across the sky over a year. The planets and Moon always appear close to this line.
Exoplanet
A planet orbiting a star other than the Sun. More than 5,000 have been confirmed.

G

Galilean moons
Jupiter's four largest moons — Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto — discovered by Galileo Galilei in 1610.
Gas giant
A large planet composed mostly of hydrogen and helium, with no solid surface. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants.

H

Heliosphere
The vast bubble of charged particles blown out by the Sun's solar wind, which surrounds the entire Solar System.

I

Ice giant
A giant planet made largely of 'ices' such as water, ammonia and methane beneath a hydrogen–helium atmosphere. Uranus and Neptune are ice giants.

K

Kuiper belt
A ring of icy bodies beyond Neptune's orbit, home to Pluto and many other dwarf planets and comets.

L

Lagrange point
A position where the gravity of two large bodies balances the orbital motion of a small object. The James Webb Space Telescope orbits the Sun–Earth L2 point.
Light-year
The distance light travels in one year — about 9.46 trillion kilometres. Used for distances to stars and galaxies.

M

Magnitude
A measure of an object's brightness. Lower numbers are brighter; the brightest stars have negative magnitudes.

O

Oort cloud
A theorised spherical shell of icy objects at the far edge of the Solar System, thought to be the source of long-period comets.
Opposition
When a planet is directly opposite the Sun in the sky, rising at sunset. Outer planets are closest and brightest around opposition.
Orbit
The curved path an object follows around another under the influence of gravity.

P

Perihelion
The point in a planet's orbit where it is closest to the Sun. Earth reaches perihelion in early January.

R

Retrograde motion
The apparent backwards (westward) drift of a planet against the stars, caused by Earth overtaking it in its orbit.

T

Terrestrial planet
A rocky planet with a solid surface. Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are the terrestrial planets.
Tidal locking
When an object's rotation matches its orbital period, so it always shows the same face to its partner — as the Moon does to Earth.
